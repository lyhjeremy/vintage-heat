An extra degree of summer is worth a fraction of a point

A growing season 1 °C warmer than a region's normal moved Wine Enthusiast scores by {{cool}} points in cool regions and {{warm}} points in warm ones. The question was whether heat helps cool places and hurts warm ones. It helped the cool ones a little, and no group shows a significant negative effect; the gap between the two is not statistically clear, and the checks weaken even the cool-region estimate.

The question

Do hot years make better wine, or only in cool places?

Wine writers talk about hot vintages and cool ones as if the weather were printed on the label. Economists have taken that seriously: Ashenfelter, Ashmore and Lalonde (1995) showed that the prices of mature red Bordeaux can be predicted from growing-season weather, with great vintages in years when the growing season is warm, August and September are dry and the previous winter wet. Jones, White, Cooper and Storchmann (2005) found that vintage ratings in most European regions rose with growing-season temperature, and for some regions modeled an optimum growing-season temperature, beyond which warmer seasons scored lower.

If the optimum idea holds across regions, an extra degree should help a cool place such as Mosel and do little or harm in a hot one such as Napa. This study puts that to the Wine Enthusiast reviews, using each region's own seasons rather than one region's history.

Data

{{nRegionsWord}} regions, {{nVintagesWord}} vintages, one weather point each

The reviews are the public winemag-data-130k-v2 set of {{nReviewsAll}} Wine Enthusiast reviews, the same file as the wine score inflation study. The file holds {{nDupes}} exact duplicate rows, which are dropped. The vintage is the first four-digit number from 1950 to 2017 in each wine's title; {{nVintage}} of the remaining reviews carry one. A region is the review's country / province / region_1 label (or the province where region_1 is empty), and it is kept if it has at least 400 reviews and 10 vintages between 2000 and 2016. {{nCatchAll}} labels that pass the counts but cover more than one climate, such as "California" and "Toscana", are dropped. That leaves {{nRegions}} regions and {{nPanel}} reviews, a list in data/regions.csv. The list was frozen before any weather was fetched and re-frozen on 29 October 2026, when dropping the duplicate rows took {{nExcluded}} of the original {{nFirstFreeze}} regions below 400 reviews; the rule did not change.

Each region gets one point: the coordinates of its Wikipedia page (an AVA's page where it has coordinates, otherwise the region's main wine town). Weather is ERA5-Land reanalysis from the Open-Meteo archive: daily highs and lows over the growing season (April to October in the north, October to April in the south). Open-Meteo returns no rain for ERA5-Land, so harvest rain comes from ERA5, the coarser reanalysis that drives it. As a check on the points, the terrain elevation Open-Meteo uses at each one was compared with the elevation its source gives. {{elevPass}} points fall within 300 m; {{elevUnchecked}} sources give no elevation; one point, the Santa Barbara County centroid, sat at 888 m in the mountains and was moved to Los Alamos, between the Santa Maria and Santa Ynez valleys.

Method

Each region against its own normal, each year against the others

For every region and season the pipeline computes the mean temperature (Jones's growing-season temperature, GST), degree days above 10 °C, days above 35 °C, and the rain in the two harvest months. Each is set against the region's 1991-2020 average, so a +1 °C season in Mosel and a +1 °C season in Paso Robles mean the same thing: one degree warmer than that place usually is. Over 2000 to 2016 the anomaly has a standard deviation of {{anomSd}} °C.

The model regresses each review's score on the GST anomaly, allowed to differ between regions below and above {{split}} °C normal GST, and on the harvest-rain anomaly. It carries fixed effects for region, vintage year, variety and taster, and its standard errors are clustered by region. The year effects matter: seasons warmed by about {{warmingDecade}} °C a decade across these regions, and anything that moved all scores in a given year is absorbed, so the weather effect comes only from regions having a warmer or cooler year than each other. {{split}} °C is the line between Jones's intermediate and warm climate groups (Jones 2006), which sort wine regions by their growing-season temperature: cool below 15 °C, intermediate 15 to 17, warm 17 to 19, hot above 19.

In the {{nCool}} regions below {{split}} °C, a season 1 °C warmer than normal moved scores by {{cool}} points (95% interval {{coolCi}}, {{coolP}}). In the {{nWarm}} regions at {{split}} °C and above it moved them by {{warm}} ({{warmCi}}, {{warmP}}). The difference, {{diff}} points, has a p-value of {{diffP}}. Treating normal GST as continuous gives {{intAnom}} points per degree at {{split}} °C and a change of {{intSlope}} for each degree of warmer normal ({{intSlopeP}}): no detectable slope.

Jones's four bands tell the same story without a clean gradient: cool {{band_cool}} ({{nBand_cool}} regions, {{band_coolP}}), intermediate {{band_intermediate}} ({{nBand_intermediate}}, {{band_intermediateP}}), warm {{band_warm}} ({{nBand_warm}}, {{band_warmP}}) and hot {{band_hot}} ({{nBand_hot}}, {{band_hotP}}). The cool band's small interval rests on {{coolBandCountWord}} regions ({{coolBandRegions}}), too few clusters for a reliable standard error. The warm band is flat, and {{slopesNeg}} of the {{nRegions}} regions taken one at a time slope downward, so the data do not rule out a small negative effect in some warm places.

For scale: review scores here have a standard deviation of {{pointsSd}} points, and a typical season is {{anomSd}} °C off its normal, which moves a cool region's scores by about {{coolPerSd}} points. Degree days give the same split ({{gddCool}} and {{gddWarm}} points per 100 degree days). Days above 35 °C add nothing once the mean is in the model ({{heatCool}} and {{heatWarm}} points per extra hot day).

Checks

The placebo is not quite zero, and region trends erase it

Next year's weather cannot change this year's wine. Put in place of the real season, it gives {{plcCool}} points in the cool group ({{plcCoolP}}) and {{plcWarm}} in the warm group ({{plcWarmP}}); next year's harvest rain gives {{plcRain}} ({{plcRainP}}). None is significant, but the cool-group placebo is about half the real estimate. Seasons are correlated from one year to the next within a region, so part of the real estimate may be a region running warm and well-reviewed for several years at once rather than the season itself. With both seasons in the model at once, the real one keeps {{jointCool}} points in the cool group ({{jointCoolP}}) and next year's takes {{plcjCool}} ({{plcjCoolP}}).

The other checks pull in both directions. Separate linear trends for each region in place of the year effects shrink both estimates to {{trendCool}} ({{trendCoolP}}) and {{trendWarm}} ({{trendWarmP}}). On the {{nPanelPrice}} reviews that carry a price, the main model gives {{priceSampleCool}} and {{priceSampleWarm}}; adding log price moves these to {{priceCool}} ({{priceCoolP}}) and {{priceWarm}} ({{priceWarmP}}). If price carried a vintage's reputation, controlling for it should shrink both. The cool estimate {{priceCoolDir}} slightly and the warm one {{priceWarmDir}}, so price does not explain the effect away. Region-vintage averages weighted by review count give {{cellWCool}} and {{cellWWarm}}; unweighted they give {{cellUCool}} and {{cellUWarm}}, the only version in which warm regions come out ahead. Dropping one region at a time moves the cool estimate between {{looCoolMin}} (without {{looCoolMinRegion}}) and {{looCoolMax}} (without {{looCoolMaxRegion}}).

Out of sample, holding out one vintage at a time and predicting each region-variety average for it, the weather terms cut the error from {{rmseBase}} to {{rmseWeather}} points, {{oosImprove}}%. They did better in {{oosBetter}} of the {{oosVintages}} held-out vintages. Weather explains {{withinR2Pct}}% of the score variation left after region, year, variety and taster.

Limits

What this cannot show

Tasters are not blind to vintage, so a score can carry the vintage's reputation; this measures what critics scored, not what a blind panel would. Wine Enthusiast reviews what producers send, and a weak vintage may be under-submitted, which would shrink every difference here. The data has no review dates, so the lag between harvest and tasting is unknown and differs by style: a Barolo reaches a critic years after a Sauvignon Blanc.

One reanalysis point stands in for each region's vineyards and slopes, and harvest rain comes from a coarser grid than temperature. That point decides the climate band: ERA5-Land puts Carneros ({{carnerosGst}} °C) and Russian River Valley ({{rrvGst}} °C) in the {{carnerosBand}} band, though both are usually called cool AVAs, and the Douro point (Alijó, {{douroPointElev}} m) sits above most Douro vineyards. The elevation check compared each point with the elevation of its town or AVA page, not with the vineyards' elevation as the project spec asked, so it catches gross errors only. Taster and region are close to the same thing: in {{oneTaster90}} of the {{nRegions}} regions one taster wrote more than 90% of the reviews, so the taster effects and region effects are hard to separate. Scores sit in a narrow band (mean {{pointsMean}}, standard deviation {{pointsSd}}), the compression the wine score inflation study documented. The variety version of the analysis, crossing the anomaly with each grape's climate-maturity group, was cut: Jones's grape groupings exist only as a chart, and assigning {{nVarieties}} varieties by reading it would have meant guessing most of them.
